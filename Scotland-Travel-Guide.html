Road Trip Intel

Driving in
Scotland

The honest truth: Driving in Scotland is one of the most rewarding parts of the trip — and also the thing that will stress you out the most on day one. These tips are from experience, not brochures.
01
Left side of the road
You'll adjust faster than you think — probably within an hour. The hardest part isn't the driving, it's the instinct when you first get in the car to sit on the wrong side. Every morning when you get in, pause and remind yourself. It's the roundabouts that catch you.
02
Single-track roads: the passing place
Most Highland and island roads are single-track — one lane, two directions. When you meet oncoming traffic, one car pulls into a "passing place" (a small widened section, marked with white diamonds or posts). The rule: the car closest to the passing place pulls in. Wave when you pass. It's a whole culture.
03
Do not trust your GPS on timing
Google Maps wildly underestimates single-track road travel time. A road that "should" take 20 minutes can take 45. Add 30–50% buffer to any rural route, especially on Skye and Mull. Also: sheep have absolute right of way and know it.
04
Download offline maps before you leave
Cell signal in the Highlands and on Skye is genuinely spotty — especially on the western coast. Download Google Maps or Maps.me for the entire Scotland region before you board your flight. You'll use it.
05
Fuel up when you see a petrol station
Don't assume there's another one ahead. On some Highland routes there are 40+ mile gaps between stations. Fill up whenever you're at half a tank in rural areas. Petrol stations in small towns may also close early.
06
The weather changes fast — and that's the point
Scottish weather is famously unpredictable, but the light it creates is extraordinary. A grey morning can turn into golden-hour perfection in an hour. Don't skip a hike because it looks overcast — mist on the Cuillin is part of the magic. Pack a waterproof layer, always.
07
Park early at the big spots
Old Man of Storr, Fairy Pools, and Neist Point all have small car parks that fill by 9–10am in season. Arrive early or arrive late (late evening light in Scotland in April/May is stunning — it stays light past 9pm). Avoid midday at the main Instagram spots.
08
Roundabouts: give way to the right
UK roundabout rule: yield to traffic already on the roundabout, coming from your right. Indicate left when you're about to exit. This feels backwards at first but becomes automatic. Practice in a quiet area before hitting a big city roundabout.
Favourite unexpected road moment: Pulling off on the A87 between Eilean Donan and Skye where there's a tiny layby overlooking Loch Duich with no one around. No marker, no sign — just an obvious spot to stop. Scotland rewards you for slowing down.

Planning the Trip

This is a full-day boat excursion that leaves from Oban or Fionnphort (Mull) and stops at both Staffa and the Treshnish Isles. The operator to know is Staffa Tours — they run combined Staffa + Lunga trips with a Zodiac landing on Lunga. Seats are genuinely very limited and the May–July departures sell out weeks or months ahead. Book as soon as your dates are fixed.

📅 Booking
  • Staffa Tours — departs Oban (easiest if you're based there). Also runs from Fionnphort and Iona.
  • Combined Staffa + Treshnish Isles day trip — around £55–65 per person (verify current pricing at booking).
  • Puffin season: mid-April to late July. Peak is May–June — birds are most active, bluebells are out on Lunga.
  • Book directly via staffatours.com well before your trip — don't assume availability when you arrive in Oban.
  • Weather cancellations happen. Have a flexible backup day built into your schedule.
🎒 What to Wear & Bring
  • Full waterproofs — top and bottom. The sea crossing is open Atlantic. Even on a good day you'll get spray.
  • Flat, grippy shoes with ankle support. Lunga is uneven, tussocky ground. No sandals, no smooth soles.
  • No perfume or strong scents. Puffins have a good sense of smell and scent can stress them at close range.
  • Telephoto lens — 200mm minimum. The puffins come within a few feet but you'll want reach for birds in flight.
  • Sea-sickness tablets if you're prone — the crossing can be lumpy.
  • Snacks and water. There's no café on Lunga.
On the Island

You reach Lunga by Zodiac inflatable — the main boat anchors offshore and the Zodiac ferries you in groups to a rocky landing. From there you're on your own for about two hours. The island is small but the clifftop above the landing holds hundreds of puffins in season. They are completely unbothered by people — you can sit within three or four feet of a burrow entrance and they'll ignore you entirely.

🐦 Puffin Behaviour on Lunga
  • Stay low, move slowly. Sit or crouch near burrows rather than standing over them. Puffins respond to perceived size.
  • Don't block burrow entrances. If a bird is waiting to land or get in, step back — they have one egg to feed.
  • Watch the sky as much as the ground. The landing-approach shot — wings hammering, feet splayed — is the one you'll remember.
  • The puffins with fish in their beaks are returning from feeding runs. Don't approach these birds — they need to get underground fast before gulls rob them.
  • You'll also share the island with rabbits — puffins take over abandoned rabbit burrows, which is why the species coexist on the same hillside.
🌊 The Thing You Weren't Expecting

Walk past the puffin colony to the far side of Lunga and look over the cliff edge. What you'll find is a guillemot colony — thousands of common guillemots and razorbills packed shoulder to shoulder on every ledge of the cliff face, a wall of seabirds from sea level up. It's extraordinary in a different way to the puffins — chaotic, deafeningly loud, almost prehistoric.

Most people on the boat don't make it this far because they spend all their time with the puffins. Walk the extra ten minutes. It's worth it.

🪨 Staffa & Fingal's Cave — The Other Stop

The same boat trip typically stops at Staffa before Lunga. Staffa is a volcanic island of geometric hexagonal basalt columns — the same geological formation as the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland. Fingal's Cave is a sea cave you can walk into from the clifftop steps, and it's genuinely extraordinary: the hexagonal columns line the walls floor to ceiling, the sea surges in underneath, and the acoustics are strange and resonant. It's what inspired Mendelssohn's Hebrides Overture. Puffins also nest on Staffa's cliffs in season, though the Lunga colony is far larger. Even if there were no puffins anywhere, Staffa alone would justify the boat trip.

The Biology
Why they look like that
The colourful triangular beak is seasonal — it fades after breeding season and the plates actually shed. Outside of April–August, puffins at sea are much drabber. The beak's bright colours are for attracting a mate, not for fishing. Their all-black-and-white body is countershading: dark on top (invisible from above), white below (invisible from beneath the water to fish looking up).
Burrow life
Puffins nest underground in burrows they dig themselves or take over from rabbits (which is why you see rabbits and puffins sharing the same hillside). Both parents incubate the single egg for ~39 days. The chick (called a puffling) is fed fish by both parents for 6 weeks, then left alone in the burrow to find its own way to the sea at night.
The fish trick
Puffins can hold fish in their beak while continuing to catch more — a unique adaptation involving a rough tongue and a hinge in the upper mandible. The world record is 83 fish in one beak. They primarily hunt sand eels, sprats, and herring. Watch them coming in to land — wings working overtime, feet splayed forward like tiny orange landing gear.
Conservation status
Listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Atlantic puffin numbers have declined sharply in parts of their range due to climate change affecting sand eel populations (their main food source), overfishing, and introduced predators on breeding islands. Scotland's remote island colonies are among the healthiest remaining populations — which is why the Treshnish Isles matter.

The Look of the Highlands & Islands

Castles reflected in lochs. Shaggy cattle on single-track roads. Medieval ruins standing open to the sky. A bagpiper in full highland dress outside a stone close. The Highlands and islands reward the slow look.

Highland Life

The animals are a feature of the drive, not just the scenery. Highland cattle on Skye are genuinely unfazed by cars — they'll stop traffic and stand in the road and dare you to argue about it.

Castles & Ruins

Scotland has more castles per square mile than almost anywhere in Europe. Some are famous. Many are roadside surprises. Kilchurn appears suddenly on Loch Awe as you drive the A819 toward Oban — no sign, no carpark, just a ruined tower reflected in still water.

St Andrews — Cathedral & Castle

The cathedral was once the largest building in Scotland. Completed in 1318, it survived four centuries before the Protestant Reformation reduced it to the atmospheric ruin you walk through today — free, open-air, genuinely extraordinary.

The Old Course

Golf was invented here. The Old Course is public — anyone can walk across the 18th fairway, across the Swilcan Bridge, past the R&A Clubhouse. You don't need to be a golfer to understand why people have been making the pilgrimage for 600 years.

Whisky Country

The landscape and the whisky are inseparable. Talisker tastes like the sea and peat of Skye because it's made from Skye's water and air. Oban tastes like the west coast because it's hemmed into a cliff above it. You don't need to tour a distillery to feel the connection — you'll taste it in the glass.

History & Terminology — Why Things Are Called What They Are

A lot of what makes the Highlands feel foreign to outsiders is just unfamiliar vocabulary. Here's the context that makes the landscape readable — what the words mean, why the geography is the way it is, and what the buildings were actually for.

💧 Loch, Glen, Strath, Ben

Loch is Gaelic for lake (or sea inlet, in which case it's a sea loch). Scotland has so many because the landscape was carved by glaciers during the last ice age — the ice scoured deep U-shaped valleys, which filled with meltwater as the glaciers retreated. Loch Ness is so deep (230m) because a glacier sat in that valley for thousands of years, grinding down.

Glen is a narrow mountain valley — the classic V or U shape between ridges. Strath is a broader, flatter river valley — wide enough for farming. Glen Coe is a glen; Strathspey (where Speyside whisky comes from) is a strath. Ben (from beinn) means mountain peak. Ben Nevis, Ben Lomond — the "Ben" tells you it's a summit.

⛰️ Munros & Corbetts

A Munro is any Scottish mountain over 3,000 feet (914m). There are 282 of them. The list was compiled by Sir Hugh Munro in 1891. "Munro-bagging" — trying to climb all 282 — is a serious pursuit that occupies people for years or decades. Completing all of them is called "compleating" (not a typo, that's the accepted spelling).

A Corbett is a Scottish mountain between 2,500 and 3,000 feet with a re-ascent of at least 500 feet on all sides — there are 222. Below Corbetts are Grahams, then Donalds (Lowland hills). The classification system is entirely serious and has its own governing body. If you meet someone in the Highlands with a well-worn OS map, they're probably bagging.

🏰 What a Castle Actually Was

The Highland clan system meant that power was local and military. A clan castle was the physical seat of a chief's authority — part fortification, part administrative centre, part symbol. In the Highlands, holding a castle meant controlling the surrounding valley and the routes through it.

The terminology matters: a tower house is the simplest form — a tall narrow fortified residence, four or five storeys, with the livestock on the ground floor and the family above. A fortified house is broader, with more domestic space and less pure military function. A true castle has curtain walls, a gatehouse, a bailey — the full defensive architecture. Most of what gets called a "castle" in Scotland is technically a tower house. Eilean Donan is a genuine castle; many of the roadside "castles" are tower houses.

Most Highland castles are in ruins because of the aftermath of the Jacobite risings — particularly after Culloden (1746), when the British government systematically dismantled clan power, including the physical buildings that expressed it.

🗺️ Reading Gaelic Place Names

Place names across the Highlands are Gaelic because Gaelic was the language of the region for over a thousand years. The prefixes unlock the landscape:

  • Inver- — river mouth or confluence. Inverness = "mouth of the River Ness." Inveraray, Invergarry — all at confluences.
  • Kil- — from cill, a church or monastic cell. Kilchurn, Kilmartin, Kilmarnock — early Christian sites.
  • Drum- — a ridge. Drumnadrochit = "ridge of the ford." Most Drum- places sit on elevated ground.
  • Bal- — a farm or settlement. Balmoral, Ballater, Ballachulish — all farmsteads or small townships.
  • Kil-/Kyle- — Kyle also means a narrows or strait. Kyle of Lochalsh (where you cross to Skye) is the narrows of the sea loch.
  • Strath- / Glen- — already above, but worth noting as prefixes: Strathclyde, Glencoe, Glenfinnan.
Edinburgh

The city you arrive into and leave from. Day 1 barely scratches it. The view from the castle battlements over the New Town to the Firth of Forth is one of the great urban panoramas in Europe.

Honest take: Edinburgh is genuinely world-class. The castle, the Royal Mile, Arthur's Seat, the closes — if you love dense layered history and urban walking, you could spend four days and not run out of things. But if that's not what draws you, one day is perfectly fine. Don't manufacture a second day just because it feels obligatory. The real pull of this trip is west and north — the Highlands, Skye, the Atlantic islands. Edinburgh is a brilliant start; it's not the destination. Get there.

🦟 The Midge Warning

The Highland midge (Culicoides impunctatus) is a tiny biting insect that swarms in warm, still, humid conditions — basically Scottish summer. They're worst at dawn, dusk, and after rain near still water. They don't carry disease but the bites are intensely itchy and they arrive in thousands. Smidge is the locally-made repellent that actually works. Buy it in any Scottish outdoor shop or pharmacy.
